## THE BIG STORY

### Florida coral rescue produces 61,000 larvae in 12 nights

Scientists working in the Florida Keys have produced 61,000 elkhorn coral larvae and 101 distinct genetic crosses in an ambitious effort to rebuild one of the state's most imperiled reef-building species. Over 12 nights, specialists from The Florida Aquarium and partner organizations collected eggs and sperm from nursery-grown corals off Tavernier. About 20,000 of the resulting larvae are headed to the aquarium's coral conservation center, with others distributed to research partners.

Some parent colonies were chosen because they survived recent extreme heat without bleaching. The team hopes to find out whether that resilience can be inherited, but a successful breeding effort is only the beginning: raising young corals and establishing them on reefs remain formidable challenges.

## WILDLIFE ENCOUNTERS

### Two whale sharks surface beside a boat off New York

Passengers on an offshore wildlife-watching trip got an extraordinary view of two whale sharks near Hudson Canyon, roughly 100 miles from New York City. The encounter took place on August 31 and was reported this week after naturalist Chris St. Lawrence and fellow passengers recorded the animals swimming close to their vessel. Despite their enormous size, whale sharks are filter feeders, not hunters of people.

The sighting is a reminder that the waters beyond New York's skyline host a surprisingly rich pelagic ecosystem. Researchers say warm Gulf Stream water and food availability can help bring these typically tropical visitors into the northwest Atlantic. A rare encounter alone, however, cannot establish a lasting shift in the species' range.

## WRECKS & REEFS

### A sunken WWII hospital ship has become a refuge for monk seals

The *Po*, a 135-metre Italian hospital ship sunk in Albania's Bay of Vlora in 1941, now supports an unexpectedly diverse marine community. Researchers surveyed the wreck on 32 dives between 2022 and 2024, using sonar and three-dimensional imagery to document the life taking hold around its hull. They recorded 151 species, including invertebrates, fish and the critically endangered Mediterranean monk seal.

The ship lies about 35 metres deep and was rediscovered by divers in 2005. Much of its surface is covered with organisms such as sponges and sea squirts, turning wartime wreckage into complex habitat. The findings underline how artificial structures can attract biodiversity, even as historic wrecks remain sensitive archaeological sites.

## OCEAN SCIENCE

### Warming seas won't make every marine animal smaller

A new study covering more than 23,000 species of marine mollusks complicates the familiar prediction that ocean warming will shrink marine life. Researchers combined millions of species records with body-size data and modeled how temperature, oxygen and food availability could affect clams, snails, squid, octopuses and related animals through 2100. Many groups are projected to become smaller, but the effects differ sharply by species and ocean region.

Under a high-emissions scenario, the team projected that some Arctic cephalopods and tusk shells could grow larger. Elsewhere, even modest declines in body length could translate into substantial losses in mass. The study highlights why ocean change cannot be captured by a single global rule of thumb.

## UNDERWATER DISCOVERIES

### A routine police dive uncovers three Roman amphorae off Sardinia

Police divers conducting an underwater safety operation near Porto Rotondo, Sardinia, noticed a fragment of terracotta on the seabed. They returned for a closer inspection and found three largely intact ancient amphorae, along with additional fragments that require specialist evaluation. The vessels have been documented, and Italy's archaeological authorities have been notified.

Early assessments suggest the containers could date from the late first century BCE to the first century CE, possibly originating in parts of Italy or Spain, but those details are not yet confirmed. The discovery shows how archaeological evidence can surface during work unrelated to excavation—and why documenting a find carefully matters more than rushing to identify it.

## ONE MORE THING

### In remote Hawaii, marine debris removal demands breath-hold skill

Even the protected reefs and atolls of Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument cannot escape discarded fishing gear and plastic carried by ocean currents. Debris accumulating in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands threatens Hawaiian monk seals, sea turtles, seabirds and coral habitats. In a new *Our Wild Lives* episode, The Wildlife Society examines the difficult cleanup work carried out by the Papahānaumokuākea Marine Debris Project.

The discussion explores how teams train for breath-hold work and remove entangling nets from sensitive environments. The setting is remote, but the lesson reaches well beyond Hawaii: marine protection on a map does not stop drifting plastic, and practical conservation frequently means painstaking work in the water.
